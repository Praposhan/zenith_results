Snoring Isn't Just Annoying — Here's What the Research Says It's Connected To

2026 · By Geethika Babu

Snoring gets treated as a punchline more often than a health signal. The cardiometabolic research tells a more serious story — one worth taking seriously without jumping to self-diagnosis.

A landmark study, and what followed

A landmark 2012 study published in JAMA by Marin and colleagues examined the relationship between treated and untreated obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) and hypertension risk, becoming one of the most cited pieces of evidence connecting the two. Subsequent meta-analyses have found a clear dose-response relationship: one pooled analysis found the odds of hypertension increased progressively with OSA severity — roughly 18% higher odds with mild OSA, 32% with moderate, and 56% with severe OSA, compared to those without the condition.

The weight connection specifically

One meta-analysis examining body weight and OSA severity found a striking dose-response pattern: a 10% increase in body weight was associated with roughly a 30% increase in the severity of breathing disruptions during sleep (measured via the apnea-hypopnea index), while a 10 to 15% reduction in body weight was associated with roughly a 50% reduction in that same measure. This is a genuinely two-way, modifiable relationship — not a fixed risk.

What this isn't

None of this is a basis for self-diagnosis. Snoring, fatigue, or a screening tool flagging elevated risk is a reason to talk to a physician and consider a proper sleep study — not a diagnosis in itself. What the research does make clear is that this is a legitimate cardiometabolic risk factor, not just a nuisance sound.

Breath to Rise exists for exactly this population — supporting the lifestyle factors within someone's control, alongside a physician's evaluation, not instead of it.
