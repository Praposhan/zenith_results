Your Phone Is Quietly Editing Your Bedtime

2 min read · PRAPOSHAN Journal

You already know screens before bed are "bad for sleep." What's less commonly explained is the actual mechanism — and how little light it takes to trigger it.

The specific wavelength your eyes are built to detect

Deep in the retina sit cells called intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells, containing a light-sensitive protein called melanopsin. These cells are most sensitive to short-wavelength light in the 460–480 nanometer range — the blue-enriched light emitted by most LED screens, including phones, tablets, and laptops. When this light hits the eye in the evening, it signals your brain's master circadian clock to suppress melatonin, the hormone that rises in the evening to prepare your body for sleep.

How little exposure it actually takes

One review found that 50% suppression of melatonin occurred at light levels below 30 lux — dimmer than typical indoor room lighting, let alone a bright phone screen a foot from your face. Controlled studies of tablet and e-reader use found that even short evening sessions measurably suppressed melatonin, delayed self-selected bedtime by roughly half an hour, and reduced next-morning alertness. Interestingly, the same research found more than a 50-fold difference in how sensitive different individuals are to evening light — meaning "it doesn't affect me" might genuinely be true for some people, while being confidently wrong for most.

What actually helps

Blue-light blocking glasses show inconsistent results across trials — some studies find a real benefit, others don't, largely due to wide variation in how well different glasses actually filter blue wavelengths. The more reliable lever is simpler: reducing evening screen exposure itself, particularly in the hour before bed.
