Are You Really a 'Night Owl,' or Is That Just an Excuse?

2 min read · PRAPOSHAN Journal

Chronotype — whether you naturally lean toward mornings or nights — gets used to explain a lot of productivity habits. The actual research is more careful about what it can and can't claim.

What a 2025 review actually found

A systematic review examining the relationship between chronotype and cognitive performance concluded that chronotype on its own does not strongly or consistently predict cognitive function in healthy adults. That's a more modest claim than the popular narrative of "night owls think better at night, period." What the review did find compelling evidence for is something called the synchrony effect — performance tends to be better when a task is scheduled to align with a person's own circadian peak, regardless of whether that peak happens to be in the morning or evening.

Why this distinction actually matters

It reframes the useful question. Instead of "am I a morning person or a night person" as an identity, the more actionable question is "when, specifically, is my own attention and decision-making sharpest" — which is measurable, individual, and doesn't require fitting into either stereotype. Two self-described night owls can still have meaningfully different actual peak-performance windows once you look at real data instead of self-report.

This is exactly the kind of individual pattern coaching is built to uncover — not a generic template, but what's actually true for one specific person's rhythm.

References
