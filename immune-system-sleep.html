Sleep Is Doing More for Your Immune System Than You Think

2026 · By Geethika Babu

"Get some rest, you're fighting something off" turns out to be more literally true than most casual advice.

The rhinovirus experiment

In a striking 2015 study published in the journal SLEEP, researchers led by Aric Prather at UCSF tracked 164 healthy adults using wrist actigraphy for a week, then deliberately exposed them to a common cold virus under quarantine conditions. Shorter sleep duration in the week before exposure predicted a significantly higher likelihood of actually developing a clinical cold — not just feeling worse, but objectively becoming infected and symptomatic. An earlier 2009 study by Sheldon Cohen's team, published in Archives of Internal Medicine, found something similarly specific: sleep efficiency below 80% predicted greater risk of illness following viral exposure.

Why sleep loss weakens defenses

The proposed mechanisms include reduced natural killer cell activity, altered cytokine production, and a blunted antibody response — sleep-deprived individuals have shown weaker antibody responses to both Hepatitis A and influenza vaccinations in other studies. The relationship also runs in both directions: poor immune function can disrupt sleep, and disrupted sleep further weakens immune function, in a loop that's easy to fall into during a demanding, high-stress period.

Recovery — one of the three domains in the ZENITH Assessment — captures exactly this kind of downstream cost that's easy to overlook until it becomes a pattern.
