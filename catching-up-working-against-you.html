Why 'Catching Up' on Sleep This Weekend Might Be Working Against You

2 min read · PRAPOSHAN Journal

Sleeping in on Saturday to make up for a rough week feels like a solution. Depending on how much you catch up, the research says it can also be part of the problem.

Where the term comes from

In 2012, chronobiologist Till Roenneberg and colleagues coined the term "social jetlag" in a study published in Current Biology, describing the mismatch between our biological clock and our socially imposed schedule — essentially, living in a different time zone on weekdays than on weekends. Their research linked social jetlag directly to increased obesity risk, treating it as a real circadian disruption, not just a lifestyle quirk.

The dose matters

More recent research using U.S. national health survey data has refined this further, finding a specific pattern: modest weekend catch-up sleep, in the range of one to two extra hours, was associated with some metabolic benefit — but catch-up sleep beyond two hours was linked to worse outcomes, including higher rates of insulin resistance and depressive symptoms. The relationship isn't a simple "more recovery sleep is always better" — it's closer to a sweet spot, with real costs on both sides of it.

The practical version

A little weekend recovery sleep appears genuinely useful. Radically different sleep and wake times between weekdays and weekends — the kind that leaves you jet-lagged every Monday — tends to work against the recovery it's meant to provide.
